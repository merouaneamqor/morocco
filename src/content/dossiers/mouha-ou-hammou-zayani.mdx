> **Evidence note.** The best evidence on this man is, unavoidably, **French military evidence** — he is documented most densely by the institution trying to destroy him. That asymmetry shapes everything below and is flagged where it bites. Amazigh oral tradition (*izlan*, *timdyazin*) is a real and largely untapped counter-archive; none of it has been consulted directly for this dossier.

## Identity

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Clan | Imazhan, within the Ayt Harkat section |
| Confederation | **Zaian** (Izayyan), Central Middle Atlas, seat at **Khénifra** |
| Office | Qaid of the Ayt Yaʿqoub half of the Zaian confederation, by Makhzen investiture under Hassan I |
| Language | Central Atlas Tamazight |
| Confederation size | French estimates of 4,000–4,200 tents under his authority. **A French estimate, not a census.** |

## The appointment — and why it is misread

By the account that circulates in the reference literature, Mouha travelled to the Tadla with his cousin Bouhassous and was presented to **Hassan I** through the marabout **Sidi Ben Dawd Sherqawi** of the Sherqawa zawiya of Boujad. Hassan invested him as qaid, with a black cloak, a detachment of sharifian infantry (a figure of 300 appears), and artillery.

**Date disputed:** 1877, 1880 and 1886 all appear in reference works of comparable authority. Unresolved.

**Interpretive point, stated as interpretation.** French Protectorate ethnography classified the Middle Atlas as *bled es-siba* — dissidence — and Mouha as an *insoumis* chief. But he held a Makhzen commission, obtained through a recognised religious intermediary, from the sultan. He was not outside the Moroccan political order; he was **inside it, in the position that order actually offered to a mountain confederation** — autonomous power ratified by sharifian investiture. What changed in 1912 was not his status but the state claiming his allegiance.

> **Terminology.** *Insoumis*, *dissident*, *rebelle* in Résidence Générale and Bureau des Affaires Indigènes usage denote an armed leader outside **French** authority. They carry no implication of illegitimacy in Moroccan terms, and reproducing them as description rather than as evidence of French filing categories is a category error.

## Relationship with the sultans

| Sultan | Relationship | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Hassan I | Invested him as qaid; Mouha sought the investiture | Established, date disputed |
| Abdelaziz | Continued autonomy; no recorded rupture | Thin |
| Abdelhafid | His daughter **Rabha** reportedly married Abdelhafid | Single-source assertion; needs corroboration |
| Moulay Youssef | Sultan in whose name France fought him after 1912 — the central irony of the Zaian war | Established |

After 1912 France waged war on him **in the sultan's name**, under the Treaty of Fez. He did not renounce the sultanate; he refused the French administration exercising it. This distinction is what French reporting collapses and what Moroccan historiography emphasises.

## The Zaian War, 1914–1921

| Date | Event | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 12 June 1914 | French occupy **Khénifra** (Henrys) | Mouha withdraws rather than defend the town |
| 13 Nov 1914 | **El Herri** — Laverdure's column destroyed | See event dossier |
| Nov 1914 | Khénifra invested; French garrison reinforced to c. 7,000 by month's end | French figure |
| May 1915 | **Sidi Sliman** — French counter-offensive under Garnier-Duplessix; c. 300 Zaian dead | French figure |
| June 1917 | Junction at **Assaka Nidji**; trans-Atlas route opened | Strategic turning point |
| 9 Aug 1918 | **Gaouz** — French losses given as 238 killed, 68 wounded | French figure |
| June 1920 | His sons **Hassan** and **Amharoq** submit to **Poeymirau**; c. 3,000 tents follow | Reportedly on his own advice |
| 27 March 1921 | Killed at Azlag n'Tazemmourt | See Death |
| Sept 1921 | Three-pronged French attack near **El Bekrit** ends organised Zaian resistance | |

**On the 1920 submissions.** The reference literature states his sons submitted *on his advice*. If true it reframes him — not a leader fought to annihilation but one who calculated that the confederation's survival required submission while he personally would not make it. **Evidence strength: single-source assertion.** It should be tested against the Bureau des Affaires Indigènes reports for Khénifra, 1920.

## Military method

- **Mobility over position.** He gave up Khénifra in 1914 without a defensive battle. Towns were French assets; the confederation's asset was the mountain.
- **Concentration only on favourable ground.** El Herri is the exception that proves it: he engaged in strength only once a small, exposed, retreating column presented itself.
- **Confederal recruitment.** At El Herri the force included Mrabtin, Ayt Harkat, Ayt Ischak and Ayt Ichkern elements — not a standing army but a coalition assembled on a specific occasion, which is why French estimates of his strength swing so wildly.
- **Constraint.** Cut off from machine guns and artillery. The French export-control effort against arms into the Middle Atlas was, by French assessment, largely effective. Captured French weapons were therefore politically as well as militarily valuable: after El Herri he **displayed captured arms to neighbouring tribes**, and recruitment rose.

## German and Ottoman contact

During the First World War Germany, operating largely through its embassy in **Madrid**, attempted to raise Morocco against France: money smuggled in (a figure of up to 600,000 pesetas per month appears in the literature), arms, advisers, Foreign Legion deserters, and propaganda in Arabic, French and Middle Atlas Tamazight. The Ottoman Empire supplied training and religious framing.

**Assessment:** by both French and later academic reading, largely ineffective — far short of the general jihad the planners wanted. But the effort is why the Zaian war is legible in **German and Spanish** archives as well as French, and it is the reason Spanish-zone material is relevant to a Middle Atlas war fought entirely in the French zone.

Key academic thread: **Edmund Burke III, "Moroccan Resistance, Pan-Islam and German War Strategy, 1914–1918", *Francia* 3 (1975), 434–464.**

## Death — investigated, not repeated

The standard statement is that he was killed on **27 March 1921** at Azlag n'Tazemmourt in a fight against forces led by his own son **Hassan**, who had submitted to the French the previous year.

The versions do not agree:

| Version | Content | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Common encyclopedic | Killed in combat with his son Hassan's forces | Widely repeated |
| French operational | Killed in a clash with a "partisan" (French-allied Berber) party during operations; France did not kill him directly | Consistent with French practice of fighting through allied contingents |
| Some accounts | "Spring 1921", no precise date | Suggests the exact date is a later fixing |
| Moroccan commemorative | Martyr's death in resistance; the fratricidal element is present but de-emphasised | |

**What is established:** he died in early 1921, in the Middle Atlas, in an armed clash, and not in a set-piece battle against French regulars. He is buried at Ben Cherro near Tamalakt, where a tomb, mosque and mausoleum stand.

**What is not established:** whether his son struck the blow, or commanded the party, or was merely present in the opposing alignment. These are three different claims and the sources do not distinguish them. **Do not treat the fratricide as a documented fact.**

**Why it matters:** the fratricide version is narratively useful to *both* sides — to the French as proof that resistance collapsed from within, to a certain Moroccan reading as tragedy that absolves the collaborating half of the family of intent. That both sides benefit from a story is a reason to check it.

**Verification target:** French operational reports and the Khénifra Bureau des Affaires Indigènes political bulletins, March–April 1921. SHD sous-série **GR 3H**. `ARCHIVAL REFERENCE NOT YET VERIFIED`.

## Assessments by nationality

**French.** Respectful and hostile at once. He is the *chef* whose destruction justified a decade of operations, and after El Herri he became the measure of Berber military capacity. Lyautey's correspondence after El Herri shows genuine strategic alarm. The residency's framing — a feudal chief holding tribes by force, whose fall would free them — is contradicted by its own reporting of how long resistance lasted after his death.

**Spanish.** Peripheral. He appears in Spanish material via the German-Madrid channel and as a comparative reference in Rif war discussion.

**Moroccan.** A national resistance figure; El Herri is a founding episode of the resistance canon. The 1991 obelisk at El Herri lists 182 Moroccan dead — and **contains errors**, giving 700 French dead (against the French count of 623) and dating the battle to 1912. A commemorative monument is not a source.

**Amazigh.** Increasingly claimed as a specifically Amazigh figure whose resistance was written out of an Arab-nationalist independence narrative. This is a live and legitimate historiographical argument, and it is also a present-day political argument. Both should be stated.

## Historical assessment

**Established:** Zaian qaid by Makhzen investiture under Hassan I; led Zaian resistance from 1914; commanded at El Herri 13 Nov 1914; his sons submitted June 1920; he died in the Middle Atlas in early 1921; organised Zaian resistance ended Sept 1921.

**Highly probable:** his authority was confederal and negotiated, not autocratic; French estimates of his strength are unreliable in both directions.

**Disputed:** appointment date; birth year; the fratricide; the claim that he advised his sons to submit.

**Unknown:** almost everything about his internal politics — how decisions were taken in the Zaian *jemāʿa*, how he financed the war, what he thought he was fighting for in his own words. **No Zaian-side written source has been used in this dossier.** Whether one exists is itself an open question worth pursuing in the Arabic manuscript collections at Rabat.
