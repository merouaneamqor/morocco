## Identity

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Lineage | Ahl Ma al-ʿAynayn; son of Muḥammad Faḍil, of the Qadiriyya-Fadiliyya |
| Role | ʿālim, Sufi shaykh, prolific author, arbitrator, city-founder, war leader |
| Foundation | **Smara** (al-Smāra), Saguia el-Hamra, from c. 1898 — a built stone town with a library, in territory of tents |
| Makhzen office | **Khalifa of the Sahara**, appointed by Hassan I, 1887 |
| Sons | **Ahmed al-Hiba** ("the Blue Sultan"), **Merebbi Rebbu**, and others — several of whom warrant dossiers |

## Why he matters, stated plainly

He is the figure through whom the Moroccan state's claim to the Sahara was exercised in the decades when it could not be garrisoned — and therefore the figure on whom a very large modern political question rests. That makes his historiography **unusually contaminated in every direction**:

| Reader | Interest |
|---|---|
| Moroccan | Proof of Makhzen sovereignty over the Sahara through investiture and *bayʿa* |
| Sahrawi nationalist | Proof of an autonomous Saharan polity that owed the sultan religious deference, not political subjection |
| French colonial | Proof that the region was ungoverned and available |
| Spanish | Proof that Spanish coastal claims faced no organised state |

All four readings use the same handful of facts. This dossier states the facts and refuses the conclusion.

## The Makhzen relationship

**Established:** Hassan I appointed him his representative over the Saharan territories by dahir. **The date is disputed — 1879 and 1887 both appear in reference literature of comparable authority.** The 1887 dating links the appointment to Spanish occupation at Dakhla; the 1879 dating places it earlier and independent of it. **This is not a trivial discrepancy:** it changes whether the appointment was a reaction to European encroachment or a prior assertion of Moroccan sovereignty, which is exactly the point at issue in the modern territorial argument. Under Abdelaziz, and more clearly under Abdelhafid, the relationship continued: he received arms and subsidies from Fez and Marrakech and acted as the Makhzen's instrument against French advance from Mauritania and Senegal.

**Disputed:** what "khalifa" delegated. Two defensible readings:

- **Delegated sovereignty** — he governed, taxed and made war *for* the sultan, within a recognised imperial framework; his authority was derivative.
- **Ratified autonomy** — the Makhzen recognised a power it did not create and could not command, in exchange for the recognition of sharifian religious primacy. His authority was his own.

The honest position is that **both are partly true and the balance shifted over time**, moving toward autonomy as the Makhzen weakened after 1900. Any source that resolves this cleanly in either direction should be read for its politics.

## Smara

Founding a stone town with a library and a mosque in the Saguia el-Hamra from the 1890s was a **political act**: it asserted permanence and statehood in a landscape European powers classified as empty. Spanish and French reporting registered it as such.

The French razed Smara in 1913. That act — the deliberate destruction of the physical proof of a Saharan polity — is worth its own event dossier.

## Resistance and the French advance

From the 1900s the French advance from Mauritania (Coppolani, then Gouraud) collided directly with his authority. The assassination of **Xavier Coppolani in 1905** was attributed by French authorities to his circle — an attribution made by the aggrieved party and requiring independent testing.

He supplied, blessed and coordinated Saharan resistance; his sons commanded in the field.

## 1910 — the march north and Tadla

In 1910 he moved north into Morocco proper. Two things are asserted about this and they should be separated:

1. **That he marched to reinforce or replace a failing Makhzen against European encroachment.** Plausible; consistent with his record.
2. **That he claimed the sultanate himself.** Reported, and *disputed*. His son al-Hiba certainly did so in 1912. Whether the father did in 1910 is not established here.

By one account he moved north with an army of around **6,000 men** — a figure of uncertain origin — and his forces were defeated by French troops under **Moinier on 23 June 1910**. He withdrew to **Tiznit**, where he died on **23 October 1910**.

Note the four-month gap between defeat and death, which the compressed versions of this story tend to elide.

## The Spanish arms question

French reporting alleged that Spain supplied him with arms through the southern coast, in breach of Franco-Spanish understanding.

**Status: claim, not fact.** It was made by France, about Spain, at a moment of Franco-Spanish competition over the southern zone. It may be true; Spain had motive and coastline. It requires **Spanish** documentation, and none has been examined here.

## The succession: al-Hiba

His son **Ahmed al-Hiba** (b. 9 September 1877, d. 1919), *al-Sulṭān al-Azraq* — the Blue Sultan — proclaimed himself sultan at Tiznit after the Treaty of Fez, entered **Marrakech on 18 August 1912** and was proclaimed there. On **6 September 1912** his army was destroyed at **Sidi Bou Othmane** by Colonel **Charles Mangin** — reported losses around 2,000 tribal fighters against very light French casualties, the standard signature of machine guns and artillery against massed cavalry. He was driven from Taroudant in January 1913, withdrew to Kerdous in the Anti-Atlas, and died there in 1919. His brother **Merebbi Rebbu** succeeded him.

The *hibiste* movement is the direct continuation of Ma al-ʿAynayn's authority and should be researched as one arc, not two.

## Historical assessment

**Established:** Qadiriyya-Fadiliyya lineage; substantial scholarly output; founded Smara; appointed khalifa of the Sahara by Hassan I in 1887; organised Saharan resistance to French expansion; moved north in 1910, was defeated, and died at Tiznit on 23 October 1910; his son al-Hiba took Marrakech in 1912 and lost at Sidi Bou Othmane.

**Highly probable:** his authority rested primarily on religious and scholarly standing, with Makhzen investiture as reinforcement rather than foundation.

**Disputed:** the nature of the khalifa relationship; **the year of his appointment (1879 vs 1887)**; any 1910 claim to the sultanate; Spanish arms supply; his birth year (1830 or 1831; one account gives c. 10 February 1830).

**Unknown:** the scale of his following, his finances, and the content of his own extensive writings — which exist, are catalogued in part, and have **not been used here**. Given that he was a prolific author, using European reports about him in preference to his own texts is a methodological failure this dossier is currently committing and flags as such.
