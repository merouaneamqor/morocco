> The single most heavily documented episode in this workspace, because it produced a **formal Spanish state inquiry** — the *Expediente Picasso* — and because it was fought under intense international press attention. It is also the episode where the numbers are least agreed.

## Why it matters beyond Morocco

The Rif war destroyed the Spanish constitutional monarchy's credibility, produced Primo de Rivera's dictatorship, and formed the officer corps — **Franco, Sanjurjo, Millán Astray**, the Legion, the Regulares — that made the coup of July 1936. Sebastian Balfour's *Deadly Embrace* is built on that argument. Moroccan history and Spanish history are here **the same history**, and treating them separately produces bad accounts of both.

## Phase 1: the collapse, June–August 1921

**Commanders.** General **Manuel Fernández Silvestre**, Comandante General de Melilla, driving an advance west toward Alhucemas Bay. General **Dámaso Berenguer**, High Commissioner. General **Felipe Navarro**, second at Melilla.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1 June 1921 | **Monte Abarrán** occupied and lost the same day |
| 17 July | Coordinated Rifian assault on the Spanish position system |
| 21–22 July | **Igueriben** falls after a five-day siege |
| 22 July | Silvestre orders **Annual** evacuated; the withdrawal becomes a rout |
| 22–28 July | Flight toward Melilla under continuous attack; c. 3,000 reach **Monte Arruit** and are besieged |
| 9 August | **Navarro surrenders Monte Arruit** on negotiated terms. The disarmed column is massacred. Roughly **60 of c. 3,000** survive |

**Melilla itself was not taken.** Why not is a genuine question: the city was effectively undefended for days. Explanations range from a deliberate political decision not to provoke total Spanish commitment, to the practical limits of a tribal coalition after a fortnight of continuous fighting and looting.

## Silvestre's death

| Version | Status |
|---|---|
| Suicide on seeing the position was lost | Most commonly stated; **not documented** |
| Killed during the retreat | Also reported |
| Body never conclusively identified | The underlying problem |

**Status: unknown.** The suicide version serves a narrative purpose — it closes the responsibility question with the responsible officer's self-execution. That does not make it false, but it is a reason for care. Record it as **disputed**.

## The Expediente Picasso

General **Juan Picasso González** was appointed to investigate; his report was delivered in **July 1922**. It is `OFFICIAL` / `PRIMARY_ARCHIVAL` and is the strongest documentary base for the whole disaster.

Findings:

- lines extended far beyond what the available force could hold;
- advance pressed without contingency for counter-attack;
- "friendly" tribes armed by Spain whose loyalty was never secure — and who in the event turned;
- positions dispersed, poorly sited, inadequately supplied with water and ammunition;
- no organised line of retreat, no reserves held back;
- all reserves committed forward to Annual, leaving Melilla stripped.

Picasso judged Silvestre's conduct ***temeraria*** — reckless — and found Berenguer and Navarro negligent.

**What happened to it is the point.** The report reached the Cortes; a responsibilities commission was formed; and **Primo de Rivera's coup of September 1923 shelved both.** Alfonso XIII later amnestied Berenguer, the only officer condemned. An official inquiry established fault and the political system prevented the fault from attaching to anyone.

## The king question

Alfonso XIII was accused of having encouraged Silvestre's advance directly, by telegram, over the heads of the High Commissioner and the government.

**Status: alleged, never proven.** No such telegram has been produced. The allegation was politically explosive in 1921–1923 and contributed materially to the delegitimation of the monarchy, and it remains **unproven**. Record it as an allegation with its political function noted, not as a fact and not as a fabrication.

## Phase 2: the Rif Republic and the war with France, 1921–1926

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 18 Sept 1921 | Rif Republic constituted |
| 1921–24 | Spanish forces confined largely to coastal enclaves; further reverses |
| 1923 | Primo de Rivera's coup; Spanish policy turns to withdrawal to defensible lines, then to a harder war |
| 1923–27 | **Chemical weapons** used — see below |
| Apr 1925 | Rifian offensive into the **French zone** on the Ouergha; Fez threatened |
| 1925 | Lyautey superseded by **Pétain**; Lyautey resigns |
| 8 Sept 1925 | **Alhucemas landing** — large-scale amphibious operation, later studied as a model |
| 1925–26 | Combined Franco-Spanish offensive, 250,000+ troops, aircraft, artillery |
| **26 May 1926** | Abd el-Krim surrenders **to the French** at **Targuist** |

**He surrendered to France, not Spain.** A deliberate choice: the French had not been humiliated at Annual and had no revenge to take, and exile was survivable where Spanish custody might not have been. He was sent to **Réunion**.

## Chemical warfare

A distinct dossier is warranted; the essentials:

| Element | Content | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Agents | Phosgene, diphosgene, chloropicrin, and **mustard gas (yperite)** | Established in scholarship |
| Dates | c. 1921–1927; mustard gas dispersed from aircraft from summer **1924** — a year before the Geneva Protocol took effect | Established |
| Authorisation | Berenguer to War Minister **Marichalar**, **12 August 1921**: he would employ such munitions *"con verdadera fruición"* — "with genuine relish" | Quoted by Balfour from Spanish archives; `Cited by named scholar`, not verified here at document level |
| Supply | Requested from Germany via the chemist **Hugo Stoltzenberg** on 20 August 1921, in breach of Versailles; first delivery 1923. Stoltzenberg later took Spanish nationality and helped build **La Marañosa** (Fábrica Nacional de Productos Químicos) near Madrid | Established in the German and Spanish literature |
| Targets | Civilian population, **zocos** (markets) and rivers — i.e. the food and water system, not military positions | Asserted in scholarship; the specific target lists need archival confirmation |
| Scale figures | c. 127 bombers; c. 1,680 bombs a day; 13 aircraft at Tablada, Seville | **Circulating figures of uncertain provenance. Treat with suspicion until sourced.** |
| Spanish official position | No formal acknowledgement. A 2007 motion in the Congress (Esquerra Republicana) was defeated 33–3 | Established |

**Key literature:** Balfour, *Deadly Embrace* (Oxford, 2002); Kunz & Müller, *Giftgas gegen Abd el Krim* (1990); the memoir of the pilot **Ignacio Hidalgo de Cisneros**, *Cambio de rumbo* — `PRIMARY_CONTEMPORARY`, and written by a man who later became a Republican air force commander, with the political interest that implies.

**Long-term claim, flagged as unresolved:** elevated cancer rates in the Rif have been attributed to the bombardments. This is asserted in Moroccan and Spanish activist literature and in some medical publications. **It is not established here** and would require epidemiological evidence this workspace has not examined.

## Unresolved questions

1. Total Spanish dead, on an agreed definition.
2. Silvestre's death.
3. Whether the royal telegrams existed.
4. The full scale and targeting of chemical use — sortie records, munition expenditure, target lists.
5. Rifian casualties, military and civilian. **Effectively unknown, and the gap is not accidental** — nobody with the capacity to count had the interest.
6. Rif Republic internal governance and finance.
