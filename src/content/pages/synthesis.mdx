> **The master document. It is not a sequence of kings.** It reads Moroccan history as an interaction between the Makhzen, the tribes, the religious authorities, armed resistance, France, Spain, the nationalist parties and foreign economic interests — and it says where the evidence runs out.

## The argument in one paragraph

Morocco did not lose its independence in 1912. It lost it in instalments, mostly financial, between 1844 and 1906, and the military conquest that followed was the collection of a debt already contracted. The instrument was not primarily the army; it was the **treaty, the indemnity, the loan and the consular protection**. Once the Makhzen could not tax — because the people worth taxing were under foreign protection, and because the customs were pledged to foreign creditors — it could not pay for an army, and once it could not pay for an army it could not enforce the taxation. That loop closed decisively at Algeciras in **1906**. Everything after is consequence.

## I. The fiscal trap, 1844–1906

The conventional story is military: Isly 1844, Tetouan 1860, Casablanca 1907, Fez 1912. The evidence points somewhere else.

| Instrument | Date | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Anglo-Moroccan commercial treaty | 1856 | Tariff ceilings; commercial penetration |
| **Treaty of Wad Ras** | **1860** | **Indemnity mortgaging customs revenue** |
| Madrid Convention | 1880 | **Protection codified**, foreign land ownership admitted |
| French loans | from 1904 | Customs receipts pledged |
| **Act of Algeciras** | **1906** | **State Bank; customs and police under European supervision** |

The **protection system** (*ḥimāya*) is the mechanism that deserves far more attention than it gets. Consulates could place Moroccan merchants and rural notables outside Makhzen taxation and jurisdiction. Hassan I called the Madrid Conference of 1880 to curb it; the conference codified it. Abdelaziz's **tertib** — a uniform, universal agricultural tax — was the last serious attempt to escape the trap, and it failed largely because a universal tax that exempts the wealthiest is not universal and cannot be legitimated.

> **The single most valuable unanswered quantitative question in this workspace:** how many protégés were there, and what proportion of taxable wealth did they represent? The consular registers exist. **Answering this would settle the central causal argument about the fall of the Moroccan state.**

## II. What the Makhzen actually was

The colonial binary — *bled el-makhzen* (governed) versus *bled es-siba* (dissidence) — is an administrative convenience that has been mistaken for a description for a century.

The evidence in this workspace points to something else: **a system of negotiated sovereignty.** The sultan's religious authority as *Amīr al-Muʾminīn* was near-universally acknowledged. What was negotiated — continuously, sometimes violently — was taxation, appointment and jurisdiction.

Three cases make the point:

- **Mouha ou Hammou Zayani** held a Makhzen commission from Hassan I, obtained through a recognised religious broker. French reporting filed him as *insoumis*. He was not outside the Moroccan order; he occupied the position that order offered a mountain confederation.
- **Ma al-'Aynayn** was made khalifa of the Sahara in 1887 — sovereignty delegated to a religious figure with his own following, because that was how the Makhzen reached where it could not garrison.
- **The Aït Atta** rotated leadership by election. There was no caïd for France to co-opt, which is precisely why Bougafer took until 1933.

**The zawiyas are the missing layer.** Sidi Ben Dawd Sherqawi brokering Mouha ou Hammou's investiture; Ali Amhaouch supplying cross-tribal legitimacy in the Middle Atlas; Ma al-'Aynayn's Qadiriyya-Fadiliyya network holding the Sahara. Religious lineages were the connective tissue between Makhzen and tribe, and this workspace has barely touched them.

## III. How conquest actually worked

Not mainly by battle. Four techniques, in rough order of importance:

**1. Manufacturing intermediaries.** The *politique des grands caïds*. **Thami El Glaoui** is the type-case: a real pre-existing house, built by his brother **Madani**, then armed and enriched by France until it held power no pre-colonial caïd could have held — because the Makhzen counterweight had been removed. The intermediary's power depends entirely on the coloniser, which is what makes him reliable, and which is why he collapsed within weeks of France changing course in 1955.

**2. Economic strangulation.** After El Herri, Henrys abandoned pursuit for ***"submit or starve"*** — blockade, crop and pasture denial, and by 1917 a military road cutting Zaian movement. This killed far more people than the battles and **generated almost no documentation**, because no French institution was tasked with recording it.

**3. Overwhelming technical superiority at the decisive moment.** **Sidi Bou Othmane**, 6 September 1912: c. 2,000 dead against very light French losses. Machine guns and quick-firing artillery against massed cavalry. Whenever Moroccan forces accepted a set-piece engagement they were destroyed; the only Moroccan victories — El Herri, Annual — came against isolated or retreating columns.

**4. Colonised soldiers.** At El Herri, 218 Algerian/Tunisian and 125 Senegalese tirailleurs died — **55% of the French dead.** Morocco was conquered substantially by other colonised people, and it later supplied tens of thousands of Regulares to Franco's war in Spain. Neither French nor Moroccan national memory has anywhere to put this.

## IV. Two Moroccos, 1930–1934

The single most useful reframing this workspace produces.

| Same years | Urban Morocco | Rural Morocco |
|---|---|---|
| 1930 | Ya Latif campaign; the nationalist movement acquires mass form | — |
| 1933 | Party organisation, press, petitions, Geneva contacts | **Bougafer** — the Aït Atta under artillery and aircraft in the Jbel Saghro |

**These were two different struggles, in two different languages, with almost no organisational contact.** The nationalist movement was urban, Arabophone, and led by Fassi notables; the armed resistance was rural, Tamazight-speaking, and fought a war of conquest that did not end until 1934. The independence narrative constructed after 1956 unified them retrospectively.

This is the substance of the Amazigh historiographical argument, and on the evidence it is correct as a description. What it does not establish — and what should not be smuggled in with it — is that the unification was a conspiracy rather than the ordinary process by which victorious movements write their own past.

## V. Why 1953 destroyed the Protectorate

The Protectorate's entire architecture was a **legal fiction**: Morocco remained sovereign, the sultan remained sultan, *dahirs* were still signed by him. That fiction is what made the system cheap — and it made the sultan's person the one component France could not replace.

France replaced him anyway on **20 August 1953**, and discovered it could depose a sultan but could not manufacture one. **Ben Arafa** was never accepted; two attempts were made on his life; the countryside and the cities rose; the Army of Liberation formed. Within two years France restored the man it had exiled and left the country.

**The consequence that structures everything after.** Independence was negotiated *after* Mohammed V's restoration and largely on the monarchy's terms. The parties and the armed resistance had done much of the mobilising; the palace collected the sovereignty. That asymmetry explains the dismissal of the **Ibrahim** government in May 1960, the 1959 Istiqlal split and the founding of the UNFP, the suppression of the **Rif rising in 1958–59** by the army of the newly independent state, and ultimately the removal of **Ben Barka** in 1965.

## VI. Continuity across 1956

The rupture is less complete than either narrative allows.

- **Personnel.** **Oufkir** — decorated French officer, then Director-General of National Security, then Interior Minister. **Bekkay** — French officer and pasha, then first prime minister. The security apparatus of independent Morocco was staffed by men the colonial army trained.
- **Method.** The FAR suppressed the Rif in 1958–59 using techniques inherited directly from the pacification.
- **Territory.** **Allal al-Fassi's** "Greater Morocco" doctrine rests on the Hassanian-era acts documented in the Phase II dossiers. **The nineteenth-century Saharan question is the twentieth- and twenty-first-century Western Sahara question.**
- **Geography.** Three administrations, two European languages, two legal traditions and radically unequal infrastructure investment between the French and Spanish zones. The north–south divide of independent Morocco is a colonial artefact and is still visible.

## VII. What we actually know, and how

**Well established.** The chronology of treaties and campaigns. French casualties in French engagements. The institutional architecture of both protectorates. Spanish command failures at Annual, because Spain investigated itself. The authorisation and supply of Spanish chemical weapons. The sequence of the nationalist movement.

**Established but contested in interpretation.** Whether reform failed on its own terms or was destroyed by the debt and protection regimes. Whether Lyautey's respect for Moroccan institutions was principle or technique. Whether the Berber dahir was a plot or an extension received as one.

**Genuinely unknown.** Moroccan casualties in almost every engagement. The civilian cost of blockade. Makhzen finances in any year. Silvestre's death. The manner of Mouha ou Hammou's. What Roosevelt said at Anfa. Where Ben Barka is.

**Withheld rather than unknown — a different category.** French files on Ben Barka. Roughly 1,800 acknowledged CIA documents. Spanish acknowledgement of systematic chemical use, refused by parliamentary vote in 2007. **These are questions with answers that someone possesses.**

## VIII. The honest statement about this workspace

It is a **source-critical synthesis of the accessible secondary and catalogue literature, with a precise map of where the primary evidence sits.** It is not archival research. No carton has been opened. No archival reference has been invented to conceal that.

Its largest single weakness: **the Moroccan-side documentary base is currently zero.** Every dossier here describes Moroccan actors substantially through European reporting about them — including **Ma al-'Aynayn**, who was a prolific author, and **Abd el-Krim**, whose memoirs exist. Correcting this is not an enhancement. It is the difference between a history of Morocco and a history of what Europeans wrote down about Morocco.

> **The question the workspace is built to answer, applied to itself:** *what do we actually know, and how do we know it?* On present evidence, most of what is confidently repeated about nineteenth- and twentieth-century Morocco is known because a French or Spanish official wrote it in a report intended to justify what he was doing.
